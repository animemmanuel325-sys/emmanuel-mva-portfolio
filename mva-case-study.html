The scenario

A fictional family medicine practice, three things landing in the inbox at once at 8:15 AM:

  1. A patient, age 67, calls reporting two days of dizziness and a mild headache, asking to be "squeezed in today."
  2. A pharmacy fax requests refill authorization for a diabetic patient's metformin. Last refill was 95 days ago; no visit on file in 4 months.
  3. A prospective new patient emails to book an appointment and asks what insurance the practice accepts.
TASK 01

Triaging the phone call

What I decided, and what I said to the patient.

Decision: same-day appointment, booked as soon as possible — not a routine slot, and not yet an ER referral.

Reasoning: new dizziness plus headache in a 67-year-old warrants same-day evaluation even without red-flag symptoms. Before booking, I ran a quick verbal check for anything that would change the routing entirely: chest pain, one-sided weakness, slurred speech, vision changes, or a fall. A "yes" to any of those would mean advising her to go to the ER or calling emergency services on the spot, instead of scheduling.

"I want to get you seen today rather than waiting — that's the safest option with the dizziness and headache together. Before I book you in, can I ask a couple of quick questions: any chest pain, any weakness on one side of your body, or trouble speaking? ... Thank you. I have an opening at 11:30 today with the doctor — does that work? If anything changes before then, especially any of those symptoms I just asked about, please go to the nearest ER rather than waiting for the appointment." WHY THIS MATTERS: an MVA doesn't diagnose — the symptom check exists to route correctly and to document that safety-netting advice was given.

TASK 02

The refill fax

Whether I approved it, and what I sent.

Decision: did not approve it directly — routed it to the provider for review first.

Reasoning: an MVA doesn't have prescribing authority, and a 4-month gap without a visit for a diabetic patient on metformin usually means labs (A1c, kidney function) are overdue. Approving on autopilot would skip the exact check that gap exists to trigger.

To the provider: "Pharmacy is requesting a metformin refill (last refill 95 days ago, last visit over 4 months ago — no A1c or renal panel on file in that window). Flagging for your review before I authorize — would you like labs ordered first, or a visit scheduled?" To the pharmacy: "Thank you for the refill request. This is currently pending provider review due to the interval since the patient's last visit — I'll follow up within 1 business day." WHY THIS MATTERS: refill requests are one of the easiest places for an MVA to overstep scope by accident. The safe default is always "route and flag," never "approve."

TASK 03

New patient email

What I wrote back, kept HIPAA-safe.

Approach: answer the logistics question directly, and keep anything specific (coverage details, symptoms, ID numbers) off email — move that to a phone call or the patient portal.

Subject: Booking your first visit Hi [Name], Thanks for reaching out! We'd be glad to get you scheduled. We accept [Insurance A, B, C]. If you'd like, I can call you to confirm your specific coverage and find a time that works — what's the best number and a couple of days that suit you? For your first visit, please bring a photo ID and your insurance card. We'll take care of the rest once you're here. Looking forward to seeing you, Emmanuel Anim, Patient Coordinator WHY THIS MATTERS: email invites informal back-and-forth, and it's easy for a patient to volunteer personal health details in a reply. Steering anything specific to a call or portal keeps the exchange compliant.

TASK 04

Fitting the appointment into the schedule

How I made room without bumping another patient.

Approach: use built-in flexibility — a same-day cancellation, a buffer/admin slot, or the end of a block — before touching anyone else's appointment.

"Checked today's schedule — there's a 15-minute buffer slot at 11:30 normally held for overflow. I placed the patient there instead of the front desk's admin catch-up, and moved that catch-up to 1:00 PM. No existing patient appointments were changed, and the afternoon lunch break stayed intact." WHY THIS MATTERS: same-day fits should come from built-in flexibility first — protecting other patients' appointments is part of the job, not an afterthought.
